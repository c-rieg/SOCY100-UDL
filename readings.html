01 / BOURDIEU

The Forms of Capital

Bourdieu challenges traditional economic theory for reducing exchange to commercial transactions aimed at monetary profit. This overlooks non-monetary resources and the ways power can operate through forms less visible than money.

01 / ECONOMIC CAPITAL

Money & Property

Capital that can be directly and immediately converted into money. It is institutionalized through property rights.

Examples: income, savings, assets, and property.

02 / CULTURAL CAPITAL

Knowledge & Qualifications

Knowledge, skills, dispositions, and qualifications that can be converted into economic advantages under certain conditions.

Bourdieu uses this concept to explain why educational achievement is not simply the result of natural talent or innate ability.

03 / SOCIAL CAPITAL

Relationships & Networks

Actual or potential resources connected to a durable network: group membership and relationships.

Examples: contacts who can provide information, support, opportunities, or access.

Cultural capital: three states

EMBODIED STATE

What a person carries

Long-lasting dispositions of the mind and body, acquired through time and personal investment.

Examples: accent, posture, tastes, ways of speaking, and cultivated knowledge.

OBJECTIFIED STATE

Cultural objects

Material cultural goods, such as books, paintings, writings, instruments, and machines.

Someone can own an object through economic capital, but using or appreciating it fully may require embodied cultural capital: the relevant knowledge and skills.

INSTITUTIONALIZED STATE

Recognized qualifications

Academic degrees, certificates, and other formal qualifications.

These give cultural competence an officially recognized value and can help translate education into opportunities in the labour market.

Symbolic Capital

A FOURTH IDEA / SYMBOLIC CAPITAL

Recognition & legitimacy

Symbolic capital is any form of capital when it is recognized as legitimate or authoritative.

Example: a degree earned in one country may not carry the same value in another if the new society's institutions do not recognize it.

02 / CAPITAL & INEQUALITY

Conversion and Reproduction

The different forms of capital can be converted into one another, but conversion takes time, effort, and investment. Economic capital is at the root of the other forms, yet money does not automatically become cultural knowledge or relationships

CONVERSION

Capital can change form

Economic resources can help someone obtain education, develop networks, or gain access to cultural institutions. These conversions require labour-time and particular investments.

REPRODUCTION

Privilege can be passed on

Families can transmit advantages not only through direct inheritance of money, but also through education, cultural familiarity, and social connections.

03 / VIDEO

Symbolic Interactionism

GEORGE HERBERT MEAD

The self develops socially

In Mind, Self, and Society, Mead argues that the self emerges through social processes. Communication develops from gestures into language, allowing people to take the perspectives of others.

“I”: the subjective, responding aspect of the self.

“Me”: the internalized attitudes and expectations of others.

Both develop through socialization and interaction.

CHARLES HORTON COOLEY

The looking-glass self

Our sense of self is shaped by how we imagine others see and judge us. We form an understanding of ourselves partly by imagining how we appear to others and interpreting their reactions.

HERBERT BLUMER

Meaning guides action

Blumer coined the term symbolic interactionism. People act toward things based on the meanings those things have for them.

Meanings arise through social interaction and are modified through interpretation.

ERVING GOFFMAN

Everyday life as performance

Goffman's dramaturgical approach compares social life to theatre. People perform roles, manage impressions, and adjust behaviour for audiences.

Front stage: the public performance presented to others.

Back region: the area where people can step away from the performance.
